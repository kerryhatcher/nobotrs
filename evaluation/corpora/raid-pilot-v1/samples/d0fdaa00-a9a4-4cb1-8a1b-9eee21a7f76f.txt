"The Personal Memoirs of Joan of Arc" is a novel in which the history of the renowned heroine is told by her valet and secretary, one of the pupils of her father, and who describes the life of the young maid from childhood at Domrémy, through her adventurous course at the head of the French army, to her tragic death at the stake. The story begins in the quiet village of Domrémy, where Joan, a simple peasant girl, had her first visions which inspired her to liberate France from the English. Louis, a childhood friend, is her loyal companion and chronicler, and records the transition from an insignificant peasant girl to a great leader of the people. As the visions increase, she convinces the dauphin Charles of the divine commission she has received to drive the English out of France and to crown him king. Despite the distrust of the court and the church, she leads the French army to successive victories which turn the tide of the war. Her success and growing influence, however, arouse the resentment and fear of the English and French authorities, and she is finally captured by the English, convicted of heresy, and sentenced to death. Louis, who has followed her through all her course, describes her last moments with great pity and affection, and with the particular emphasis that she remained courageous and faithful to her last breath. The history is not merely one of war and politics, but of faith, of friendship, of the life of the soul. Through Louis's eyes we see her as a kindly, brave and deeply spiritual woman, whose strange life continues to inspire long after her death.
