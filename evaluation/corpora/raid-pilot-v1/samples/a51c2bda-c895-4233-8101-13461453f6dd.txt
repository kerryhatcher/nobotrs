Since the 1960s, Europe’s chances of winning medals at upcoming international sports competitions have been unexpectedly high, with its rising level of talent and the resulting growth in resources. In the field of athletics, the world has witnessed the rise of several promising young athletes, such as Sweden’s Armand Duplantis in pole vault and Britain’s Dina Asher-Smith in sprinting. The recent Diamond League meet, in which both won gold medals, heralded the possibility of European dominance at the upcoming Tokyo Olympic games. In swimming, Hungarian Katinka Hosszu, Italian Federica Pellegrini, and Russian Yevgeny Rylov have all showed promise in recent competitions. For that matter, medal prospects are high not only in the usual field of track and field, but also in the new areas of skateboarding and climbing, which will be introduced into the Olympic program in Tokyo. The world’s number-one skateboarder is French, while Britain’s Sky Brown, a ten-year-old, is one of the world’s best climbers. Moreover, by investing in high-tech training facilities and in high-level coaching, European athletes are providing a level of preparation unparalleled in other regions. Biomechanics analysis, the use of virtual reality, and other technological advances are enabling athletes to learn about their own bodies and make them more aware of their weaknesses and strengths, which all help them to prepare for international competition. In short, in the face of these factors, Europe’s prospects for bringing home a large number of medals have become much better, marking the beginning of a new trend for the continent. We do not yet know whether Europe’s athletes will be able to exploit these advantages and turn them into victories, but the current situation is undoubtedly encouraging for European sports.
