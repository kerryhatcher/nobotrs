> This talk will discuss recent work in developing reduced-order models (ROMs) to approximate transient response of thermal/fluid systems with an emphasis on the coupling between thermal and structural dynamics. Such problems arise throughout industry, such as heat spreading in integrated circuits or aerodynamic loading of aircraft wings exposed to high engine temperatures during takeoff. Of particular interest are ROMs that leverage physical insights into model reduction, rather than purely algorithmic approaches. This enables rapid application development while providing a means for exploring additional physics as well as robustness bounds on ROM approximation. Results are presented in two contexts. First is modeling the structural impact of a rapidly heating surface driven by phase change using high fidelity time-domain computational fluid dynamics data. Using these results we construct physically-motived ROMs based on averaging techniques applied to localized regions near the contact interface together with Galerkin projections onto basis functions that allow us to exploit inherent low-dimensional structure that occurs when material instabilities develop at different spatial scales under different boundary conditions [4]. These approximations form the foundation of faster solver algorithms and can also used to assess uncertainties associated with both discretization and averaging assumptions, which often lead to larger errors relative to the underlying computational physics. Here the ROM accuracy may even exceed that of standard coarse grained finite element discretizations, allowing it to serve as a surrogate model across multiple simulation settings without retraining. Secondly, ROM techniques have been developed that explicitly account for both frequency dependent dynamics due to the internal mechanical properties of piezoelectric thin films used for microscale thermophonics applications [3] and transduction phenomena resulting from spatially varying electrical potentials through the film thickness that enable strong electromechanical couplings.
