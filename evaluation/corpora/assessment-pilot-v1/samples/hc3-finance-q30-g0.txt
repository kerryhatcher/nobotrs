American depositary receipts (ADRs) are financial instruments that represent shares of a foreign company's stock that trade on a U.S. stock exchange. The price of an ADR is based on the price of the underlying foreign stock, as well as supply and demand for the ADR itself. The price of an ADR may also be affected by various market factors, such as the performance of the foreign company, the state of the global economy, and the strength of the U.S. dollar.ADRs are typically issued in the U.S. by banks or other financial institutions, which act as depositaries. These depositaries hold the underlying foreign shares and issue ADRs to represent them. The ADRs are then listed on a U.S. stock exchange and traded just like regular U.S. stocks.There are two main types of ADRs: sponsored and unsponsored. Sponsored ADRs are issued by the foreign company itself and are typically easier to trade because the company is involved in the process. Unsponsored ADRs are issued by a third party, such as a bank or financial institution, and are not supported by the foreign company. These ADRs may be less liquid and harder to trade than sponsored ADRs.
