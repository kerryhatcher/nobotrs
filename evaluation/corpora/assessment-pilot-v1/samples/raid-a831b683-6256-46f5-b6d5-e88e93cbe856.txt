> A distributed system stabilizes if it reaches an agreed state in which any further interaction remains within that state, and can thus safely be ignored by observers, while this behavior can only depend on local observations after some initial unpredictable transient period caused by faulty or noncooperative behavior during startup. We develop a framework to formalize various notions of bounded (stability) intervals required for certified self-stabilizing computations under Byzantine adversaries affecting at most $t$ nodes each acting independently but arbitrarily once per interval. This yields an optimal synchronous certification protocol using one bit per node and requiring only two rounds; we show that its correctness crucially depends on our notion of “intervals.” On a general distributed model, we construct a two-dimensional array implementing a dictionary with $k^n$-certified stability, matching a previously known single-dimensional bound [Fortune et al., PODC’90]. Moreover, we obtain an exponentially better bound as soon as we assume a suitable network structure—an $O(\log k)$-bound over all pairs of a toroidal ring with degree-$k$ connectivity—showing an intimate coupling between these parameters. Our main technical contribution consists of generic constructions based on expander graphs mapping them onto rings and lattices respectively that guarantee such bounds over every pair provided there exist sufficiently many vertices with large degree. An important application is provably-optimal broadcast protocols in certificate complexity and optimality proofs of fast matrix multiplication algorithms based on exponential tilings on finite fields. Lastly we also address fault tolerance issues due to network partitions using leader elections and give lower bound arguments concerning their relationship to certifiable distributed atomic read/write registers.
