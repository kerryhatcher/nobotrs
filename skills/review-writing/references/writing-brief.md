# Writing guide

Write for the reader's purpose and preserve the author's meaning and voice.

## Before you write

Establish purpose, audience, supplied facts, desired action and venue. Ask for missing evidence that the argument needs. Separate facts from assumptions and unresolved questions. Use only the author's actual experience and sources.

## While you write

Lead with the useful point. Use concrete nouns and direct verbs. Explain relationships in connected prose; use lists when items are parallel or sequential. Keep qualifications, uncertainty and attribution attached to their claims. Use terminology the audience needs, including technical words when precise.

Let sentence and paragraph length follow the thought. A single punchy fragment can work; repeated fragments may interrupt the argument. Lists, dialogue, deliberate repetition and em dashes can all be justified. Structural patterns are editing prompts whose utility has not yet been validated.

## Revising an existing draft

Read the whole passage first. Resolve unclear content before structure, rhythm, punctuation and vocabulary. For each suggested edit, identify what a reader could misunderstand, a possible improvement and why keeping the original might be right.

Preserve names, numbers, dates, link targets, quotations, code, attribution, negation, approximate quantities and modal verbs such as “may.” Check changes against sources; matching tokens cannot establish semantic fidelity. Record author-approved factual corrections and unresolved questions. Do not substitute invented detail for a generic claim: ask for real evidence or cut the claim.

Use `nobotrs review` for optional offline prompts. Accept or dismiss findings in context. Read aloud to check whether the draft sounds like the author and whether transitions make sense.

## When to stop

Stop when priority issues are resolved or consciously accepted and fidelity questions are answered. Bound revision passes. If another pass makes no substantive improvement, return remaining questions to the author. Zero findings and detector scores are not writing objectives.

## Reader-first and search

For web publication, Google's guidance emphasizes helpful, original, people-first content and experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trust (E-E-A-T). It asks creators to “give people context about how it was created.” Explain substantial automation when readers would expect it, and follow venue disclosure requirements. Use accurate creator details.

See [helpful content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content), [generative AI guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/using-gen-ai-content) and the [March 2024 spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2024/03/core-update-spam-policies). Generating many pages without added reader value risks scaled content abuse. Reader-serving edits support these goals; they do not guarantee search rankings.

## Never do this

Never fabricate experience, a persona, credentials, sources, names or numbers. Never insert errors or distort facts to seem human. Never iterate against a detector score. Decoding settings change surface statistics, not substance; revise for meaning instead.
